Spread, Commission and Slippage: The Hidden Cost of Trading Gold

Every gold trade pays the spread, any commission and some slippage. On one lot, a 30-cent spread costs $30 and a commission of $7 per lot adds $7. If the stop is only $3 away ($300 at risk), costs eat over 12% of that risk before the trade does anything.

YeliasPublished 27 November 20262 min read

Three costs, paid every time

  • Spread: the gap between the buy price and the sell price. You pay it the moment you open: a trade starts slightly in the red by the spread.
  • Commission: a fee per lot charged by some account types, usually in exchange for a tighter spread.
  • Slippage: the difference between the price you expected and the price you got, most often during fast moves and news.

All three are small compared with a big move. They are large compared with a small stop, and they happen on every trade, so they grow with how often you trade.

Made-up example, 1 lot of goldAmount
Spread of $0.30 (100 oz per lot)$30
Commission, round trip$7
Slippage of $0.20 on the way out$20
Total cost of one trade$57

What to do about it

  1. Count costs in every backtest. A test with zero spread is not a test of the real world.
  2. Look at cost per trade next to the average win. If costs are a large share of the average win, small errors in the estimate can flip the result.
  3. Avoid trading around the rollover and big news, when spreads widen.
  4. Compare accounts on total cost, spread plus commission, not on the headline spread alone.
Why it matters for robots

A robot that makes many trades a day pays the cost many times. Always ask for results after costs, over a long period, and prefer live results to a backtest with ideal fills.

Frequently asked questions

Is a raw-spread account better?

It depends on how often you trade. A raw spread plus commission is often cheaper in total for active traders, but you must add the two together to compare accounts.

When is slippage worst?

Usually during major news releases and in the thin hours around the daily rollover, when few orders are available at each price.

Can I avoid costs completely?

No. The aim is to keep them small compared with the size of your average move and to make sure your testing includes them.
