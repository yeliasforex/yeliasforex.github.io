Loss Aversion and the Disposition Effect: Why We Cut Winners Early

Behavioural research shows that losses hurt more than equal gains please, a tendency called loss aversion. In trading it shows up as the disposition effect: selling winning positions too early to lock in a gain and holding losing positions too long in the hope of getting back to even. Pre-set rules are the main defence.

YeliasPublished 24 November 20262 min read

What the research found

In the late 1970s Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky described prospect theory: people judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, and a loss feels roughly twice as strong as a gain of the same size. In 1985 Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman applied the idea to investors and named the disposition effect: the tendency to sell winners too soon and keep losers too long.

How it looks on a trading screen

A trade is in lossWait, it will come backA small planned loss becomes a large one
After a lossMake it back quicklyBigger size and worse decisions

Add this up and the average win shrinks while the average loss grows, which damages the reward-to-risk ratio and lowers expectancy. See risk-reward and break-even win rate.

Rules that work against the feeling

  1. Set the stop and the target before you enter, while you are calm.
  2. Use a trailing rule for winners, so exits come from a rule and not from nerves.
  3. Never widen a stop. You can move a stop to reduce risk, never to increase it.
  4. Review by R. Looking at results in multiples of risk makes the damage from these habits obvious. A journal makes it visible.
  5. Let a robot do the exits, if you cannot. A rule that does not feel fear applies the plan every time.
The honest part

Knowing about the bias does not remove it. What works is a rule written in advance that you follow even when it feels wrong.

Frequently asked questions

Is loss aversion the same as fear?

Related but not identical. It is a measured tendency to weigh losses more heavily than gains of equal size.

Why do I hold losing trades?

Because closing the trade turns a possible loss into a certain one, and that feels worse. Waiting feels like keeping a chance alive.

Can a robot avoid these biases?

It does not have them, but its rules can contain them if the person who wrote them did. And a person can still override it, which is where the bias returns.
