How to Keep a Trading Journal That Actually Improves Your Results

A trading journal records each trade's reason, size, stop, result and your state of mind, so that you can review facts instead of memories. Reviewing weekly and looking for repeated mistakes, such as moving stops or trading after a loss, is one of the cheapest and most reliable ways to improve.

YeliasPublished 10 November 20262 min read

Memory is a bad record

Most traders remember their best trades and forget their worst habits. A journal replaces how it felt with what happened. Over a few months it shows patterns that no single trade would reveal.

What to write down

Before the tradeAfter the trade
Date and time, instrument, directionExit price and time
Reason for the trade in one sentenceResult in money and in R (multiples of risk)
Entry, stop, target and sizeWas the plan followed? Yes or no
Amount at risk as a percent of the accountWhat you would change
How you feel: calm, rushed, angry, boredScreenshot of the chart, if useful

The weekly review

  1. Add up the week in R, not only in money.
  2. Mark each trade as 'followed the plan' or 'broke the plan'.
  3. Compare the two groups. Many traders find the broken-plan trades account for most of the losses.
  4. Find one mistake that repeated and write one rule to prevent it.
  5. Check the feelings column. Do losses cluster after anger, boredom or a previous loss?

Mistakes the journal tends to reveal

  • Moving or removing a stop-loss in the middle of a trade.
  • Trading larger after a loss to 'get it back'. See FOMO and revenge trading.
  • Taking setups that do not match the plan because the screen was quiet.
  • Cutting winners early and letting losers run, a pattern explained in loss aversion in trading.

A journal also protects you from the most common trap in improvement, which is changing your strategy because of one bad week. When the numbers are in front of you, it becomes easier to tell the difference between a real problem, such as breaking your own rules, and ordinary variation, such as a normal losing streak. It turns a vague feeling that something is wrong into a specific thing you can fix.

Start tiny

A simple spreadsheet and two minutes after each trade is enough. A journal you keep for a year beats a perfect one you drop after a week.

Frequently asked questions

What should I measure in a journal?

Results in multiples of risk, whether you followed your plan, and your state of mind. These three show most of what matters.

Does a robot need a journal?

Yes, a lighter one. Note when you changed settings, paused it or intervened, and what happened next. Interfering is one of the most common causes of worse results.

How long before it helps?

A few weeks gives early patterns. After about 50 to 100 trades the picture is much clearer.
