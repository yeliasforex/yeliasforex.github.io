It looks like a pattern. Is it?

On 7 November gold rose in 7 of 15 years (47%), with an average move of -0.09% and a median of -0.03%.

To judge it fairly, we did the same count for every calendar date. For nine dates in ten, the share of up years fell between 33% and 73%. This date's 47% ranks above 24% of all dates. With only 15 years per date, ordinary luck produces records like this all the time.

Not a signal

Seasonality stories are popular and rarely survive a fair test. Picking the dates that look best after the fact is a classic way to fool yourself. We publish these as history, not as a reason to trade.

How to test a calendar idea properly

  1. Fix the rule before you look. Decide the date, direction and holding period in advance.
  2. Compare against every other date, as we did above, not against a coin flip.
  3. Count the tests you ran. Try 365 dates and about 18 will look special by luck alone.
  4. Check costs. A tiny average edge disappears after spread and slippage.
  5. Test on data you did not use to find it. If it only works in the sample you mined, it is noise.

What to use instead

  • Today's volatility, which we measure daily: see the latest gold outlook.
  • Risk per trade, because it decides survival: position sizing and risk of ruin.
  • The news calendar, which really does change how wide a day is: how to read NFP and CPI.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a seasonal pattern in gold?

Some months and dates look stronger in the past, but with only 15 years per date a fair test shows most such patterns are within what chance produces. We treat them as history, not as signals.

Does gold usually rise on 7 November?

In the last 15 years gold rose on 7 November in 7 of them. But for nine calendar dates in ten the share of up years sits between 33% and 73%, so this is within normal chance.
