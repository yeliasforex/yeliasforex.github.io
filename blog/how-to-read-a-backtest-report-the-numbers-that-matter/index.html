How to Read a Backtest Report: The Numbers That Matter

In a backtest report, look first at the period tested, the maximum drawdown, the number of trades, the profit factor and how the result was split across months and years. A large net profit with a small trade count, a short period or a deep drawdown is not a strong result.

YeliasPublished 12 November 20262 min read

Start with the setup, not the profit

The big green number at the top of a report is the least informative thing on it. Before you look at profit, check what was tested.

  • Period: how many years, and did it include calm and wild markets? One good year proves little.
  • Data quality: was it tested on real tick data or on approximated data? Real ticks are closer to what you will meet live.
  • Costs: did the test include a realistic spread, commission and slippage? A test with perfect fills overstates results.
  • Settings: were the inputs the same as the ones sold or published?

The numbers that matter

NumberWhat it tells youWhat to watch for
Maximum drawdownThe deepest fall from a peakCan you live with it? See drawdown maths
Total tradesHow much evidence there isUnder a few hundred is a thin sample
Profit factorGross profit divided by gross lossNear 1 is fragile; very high with few trades is suspicious
Average win and lossThe shape of the resultsA small average win with a big average loss is a warning
Longest losing streakHow hard it is to sit throughWould you keep running it?
Results by monthWhether profit is spread or concentratedOne huge month can carry the whole report

Questions that expose a weak report

  1. What happens if the spread doubles? A fragile system fails; a robust one slows down.
  2. What do the equity curve and the drawdown look like? Smooth lines from a short period can hide risk.
  3. Was it optimised on the same data it is shown on? If yes, read overfitting explained.
  4. Is there live proof? A verified live account over months is worth more than any simulation.
The most common trick

Showing a short, perfect-looking window. Always ask for the full period and the monthly breakdown, and be cautious if either is missing.

Frequently asked questions

How many trades do I need to trust a backtest?

More is better. As a loose guide, a few hundred trades across several market conditions gives far more confidence than a few dozen.

Is a higher profit factor always better?

Not necessarily. Very high values from a small number of trades usually mean luck or over-fitting. Look for a moderate value with many trades.

Can a backtest predict live results?

It can show how a strategy would have behaved on past data. Live results are usually worse because of costs, fills and changes in the market.
