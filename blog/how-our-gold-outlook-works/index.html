How Our Gold Outlook Works: The Method, the Maths and Its Limits

Our outlook finds past days that looked like today and counts what happened next. It is tested on 3,595 past days, every live forecast is scored in public, and the honest result is that ranges and level odds are reliable while direction is close to the long-run average.

The idea in one paragraph

Nobody can see tomorrow's gold price, but history can tell us how far gold usually moves in conditions like today's. Each morning the program describes the market with five numbers, finds the days in our 16-year history that looked most alike, and counts what happened over the next one and two trading days. That count becomes the odds, the likely range and the chance of touching a level.

The five numbers that describe a day

MeasureWhat it captures
Volatility rankHow wild the last 20 days were compared with the previous two years
20-day trendHow far price sits above or below its 20-day average, in units of a normal daily move
5-day trendThe move over the last five days, in the same units
Last-day moveHow big yesterday's move was compared with normal
Range positionWhere the last close sits inside the last 10 days' high-low range

From similar days to odds

  1. Find the 250 most similar past days, using only days whose outcome was already known at the time.
  2. Look at what followed: the close one and two trading days later, and the highest and lowest prices in between.
  3. Count. The share that ended up, flat or down gives the direction odds; the spread of closes gives the range; the highs and lows give the chance of touching a level.
  4. Pull toward the long-run average. With a limited number of similar days, raw counts can look more certain than they are, so the odds are blended with the all-history average.
  5. Log it. The forecast is saved before the day starts and scored when the day closes.

What the back-test found

Before trusting the method, we ran it over 3,595 past trading days, each time using only what was known that morning. Three results matter:

The honest summary

The method is good at how far and poor at which way. That is useful for sizing stops and trades, and it is why every outlook leads with the range and treats the direction odds as a small tilt.

What the model does not know

  • Scheduled news. It does not know that a jobs report or an interest-rate decision is due. See how to read news data.
  • Positioning, sentiment and geopolitics. None of these are inputs.
  • A regime it has never seen. In a market unlike anything in the data, the similar days are not very similar. The outlook flags this as an unusual setup.
  • Costs. Spreads and slippage are not modelled, because this is not a trading system.

How we keep ourselves honest

  • Each forecast is saved before the day starts and cannot be overwritten by our software.
  • Every forecast is scored automatically and shown on the public scorecard, wrong ones included.
  • No number in any post is typed by hand: it comes from code. A post is held back if its checks fail.
  • The AI only writes a short note around the numbers, and the note is thrown away if it contains a number it was not given.

Frequently asked questions

Is this a trading signal?

No. It is educational research. The direction odds are close to the long-run average on most days, and we say so every time.

How many past days does it use?

It looks at about 4,000 trading days since 2010 and uses the 250 most similar to today for each forecast.

Why not use machine learning?

We tested a simple, transparent method first. It found almost no direction edge, which is itself the lesson: more complexity would mostly add overfitting.

Sources and method

  • Daily XAUUSD bars from our own MetaTrader 5 history, 2010 to today. Back-test: 3,595 days, k=250, scored with the Brier score against the long-run average.
